# GOOD MOURNING
## A six-part limited series · 6 × 10 minutes

**Genre:** dystopian workplace black comedy / intimate family drama.  
**Tagline:** Your loss is important to us. Please remain cheerful.  
**Logline:** In a city where emotional insurance suspends grief after every bereavement, a claims adjuster discovers her dead mother's comfort messages have been fabricated—and must help her estranged sister return an entire neighborhood's right to mourn without abandoning the people who still need relief.

**Expanded writing package:** [42-scene treatment, six complete first-draft screenplays, and continuity ledger](README.md). The expanded package governs detailed chronology and resolves the earlier distinction between a deceased person’s archive, a survivor’s clinical care, and household certification.

**The question:** If suffering can be postponed, who gets to decide when it returns?

## 1. The society and its ideological bargain

After an epidemic, emergency clinics offered a fictional neural treatment called **the Hold**, which dampens access to a recently activated bereavement response. It does not remove the factual knowledge of a death. People could sleep, work, and care for children while deciding when to begin a supervised release. It was genuinely useful.

Twenty-eight years later, the municipal insurer **WellSpring** runs the Hold through employers, housing cooperatives, and clinics. Essentials are publicly guaranteed at a modest level, but rent supplements and paid recovery leave depend on a household's Stability Certificate. Employers reward certificate compliance; landlords price uncertainty. Citizens technically have the right to refuse treatment, but many cannot afford the consequences.

The original emergency subsidy pays providers **per active Hold per month**. Long treatment generates revenue; successful release does not. A whole economy has learned not to call postponement a failure.

Public slogan: **“Feel better. Together.”** Private operational metric: active-account retention.

### Life in the city

- Pastel appointment booths sit inside supermarkets. A pharmacy sells condolences in three approved emotional intensities.
- Everyday grief has become a paperwork event. A widow can declare a “temporary reduction in sparkle.”
- Funeral leave is thirty minutes because treatment allegedly eliminates prolonged need. Actual funerals still occur; most families remember the formalities more than the feeling.
- Wealthy clients buy private, unhurried release retreats. Poor clients renew monthly and return to work.
- The underground is not a glamorous resistance. It consists of unscheduled kitchens where people sit with someone who cannot stop crying.

## 2. Non-negotiable world rules

1. **The Hold is narrow.** It dampens the affect associated with a specified loss. It cannot erase political knowledge, love, general sadness, or the factual memory of a death.
2. **It requires a monthly clinic renewal.** A missed appointment produces a gradual rebound over several days, not instant madness. Repeated renewals postpone adjustment; they do not build a transferable energy reserve.
3. **Release is a staged clinical process.** It can be difficult and requires support, but untreated grief is not itself a public-safety emergency. There is no magic device that safely makes everybody feel everything at once.
4. **Comfort messages are separate software.** Families once recorded messages before anticipated deaths. WellSpring now synthesizes unrecorded messages and passes them off as authentic. The messages cannot predict events; they are built from account data.
5. **Clinical consent and subsidy consent are legally distinct.** Existing law protects treatment refusal. WellSpring has quietly treated refusal as voluntary withdrawal from housing and leave support. The story's legal lever is that unlawful linkage—not a newly invented right.
6. **Evidence cannot heal anyone.** Exposing fraud enables consent and accountability, but ongoing treatment and wages still have to be funded.
7. **The institution can retaliate economically, not telepathically.** It controls schedules, employer notices, and account access. Private kitchens remain private unless someone reports them.

## 3. Principal characters

**Absent character:** Rena Venn, their mother. She was loving, impatient, and angry about her illness. Her real handwriting and imperfect final recording resist the sanitized maternal saint the company manufactures.

### Relationship spine

Mara and Isha begin as manager and managed problem. In episode 2, each catches the other in a comforting lie. In episode 3 they cooperate operationally but split over consent. In episode 4 both confess what they did, without earning immediate forgiveness. Episode 5 makes each defend the other's inconvenient truth. In episode 6 they can finally share a room without one trying to regulate the other's feelings.

## 4. Season causality

A reused phrase exposes a fake message → Mara audits its source → the audit reveals deliberate release cancellations → the sisters organize a legal, voluntary release session → financial retaliation forces a choice between exposure and continuity of care → tenants vote a capped reserve-funded housing bridge while disputing administration fees in escrow and continuing clinical payments → staff and residents force a documented hearing before automatic renewals → legal and clinical consent are separated → grief becomes possible, not compulsory.

**Clock:** seven days until the building's annual group certificate renews. Renewal locks its subsidy arrangement for another year. This is a local building victory with documented evidence for wider cases, not the overnight overthrow of the city's economy.

## 5. Six episodes

### Episode 1 — “Sorry for Your Convenience”
**Objective:** Mara must resolve her mother's archive account and secure Isha's household certification before the group deadline. **Local outcome:** she postpones the signature and opens an audit instead.

- **0:00–1:30 — The service transaction.** Mara handles an angry widower whose automated condolence calls arrive during his night shift. She fixes the call schedule without questioning the calls. The joke lands; his relief is real. Her promotion depends on renewals, established on a visible team board.
- **1:30–3:30 — Family as casework.** At Isha's canteen, Mara brings a tablet to dinner. Isha refuses to sign. Their mother died eleven months ago; both know this. Mara calls a release appointment “an avoidable disruption.”
- **3:30–5:30 — Comfort product.** Mara plays a supposedly recorded maternal message: “Put the kettle on before the courage goes.” A second customer receives the identical intimate line in her own mother's voice, with a different synthesized delivery. A template watermark flashes in Mara's staff console.
- **5:30–7:45 — Choice, not glitch.** Mara asks Len to identify the file. He recommends closing the duplicate ticket: the customer felt better. Mara secretly compares creation dates and finds the message was generated after Rena died.
- **7:45–10:00 — First breach.** She removes Isha from her daily completion list, costing herself a perfect score, and opens her own release history for Rena's death. Twelve release slots, including reschedules, were canceled by the provider over eleven months. **Hook:** the cancellation author is Pell, the physician Mara trusts.

**Character turn:** Mara chooses an uncomfortable fact over a reassuring performance. **Image:** two different voices carrying the same supposedly personal script.

### Episode 2 — “A Small Administrative Sadness”
**Objective:** determine whether Mara's canceled release slots were clinically justified. **Local outcome:** Mara establishes deliberate financial obstruction, not medical error.

- **0:00–1:30.** A mandatory staff exercise asks everyone to describe a disappointing sandwich as a “growth opportunity.” Len wins. Mara slips into the archived scheduling console during the exercise.
- **1:30–3:30.** Pell shows her legitimate epidemic-era cases and a patient who still chooses the Hold. His defense is powerful: withdrawal of care can be cruel. Mara notices that Mara's “clinical” cancellations cite staffing codes rather than symptoms.
- **3:30–5:30.** Suri produces their building's original contract: treatment refusal must not terminate rent assistance. Mara compares it with an internal revenue dashboard. Release slots disappear whenever retention falls below target.
- **5:30–7:45.** Isha is exposed by a renewal receipt Mara finds while searching Rena's papers. Isha replies: “I wanted one night without it. I didn't want a subscription.” She shows Mara a real recording in which their mother is angry, not soothing.
- **7:45–10:00.** Mara requests a small, supervised release session using the contract's consent clause. Pell denies staffing. She books independent licensed cover through Suri's old care network. **Hook:** an automated employer notice threatens unpaid absence for every participant.

**Character turn:** Isha's refusal is revealed as a demand for choice, not invulnerability. **Sound:** the real recording includes a cough the synthetic version edited out.

### Episode 3 — “Terms and Conditions Apply”
**Objective:** hold one voluntary session without costing participants their housing or wages. **Local outcome:** the session works, but exposes the institutional retaliation mechanism.

- **0:00–1:30.** Residents sign paper consent at the canteen. One asks if she will be required to cry. The independent clinician says no. Isha accidentally advertises the event as liberation; Mara makes her correct the language.
- **1:30–3:30.** Len arrives as compliance observer and recognizes his father's neighbor. He sees people freely choosing different options. A resident keeps treatment; another starts a gradual release plan. Neither is punished by the organizers.
- **3:30–5:30.** Mara sits with a man who says nothing for two full screen minutes across the sequence. She nearly fills the silence with advice, then stops. The emotional centerpiece is her not fixing him.
- **5:30–7:45.** WellSpring freezes the building's group certificate, blocking the next subsidy transfer. Pell calls it a temporary verification measure. Suri identifies its actual contractual effect: seventeen households will face rent arrears within days.
- **7:45–10:00.** Isha wants to publish every account file. Mara stops her: those files disclose residents' losses without consent. They release only redacted staffing and billing evidence. **Hook:** Len's dashboard labels the building “unprofitable recovery cluster”—Mara helped design that classification.

**Character turn:** Mara cannot remain the innocent investigator; Isha cannot confuse exposure with consent.

### Episode 4 — “We Value Your Feedback”
**Objective:** prevent the subsidy freeze from forcing frightened residents back into automatic renewal. **Local outcome:** residents secure a short bridge, and Mara confesses her role publicly.

- **0:00–1:30.** The redacted story attracts a sponsor offering branded “authentic mourning experiences.” Isha throws him out, then regrets losing the money. Satire targets the sale of rebellion itself.
- **1:30–3:30.** Suri proposes escrow: the cooperative pays its clinical bills but temporarily withholds the separate administrative fee under an existing dispute clause. A resident accountant checks the numbers on screen. A separately voted 5,100-credit cooperative reserve allocation buys ten days of housing support; escrow stays untouched. This is a bridge, not a revolution.
- **3:30–5:30.** Mara admits at the tenant meeting that her classifications enabled the freeze. She offers records and her savings without asking for leadership. Several residents reject her; the group still votes for escrow by a narrow margin.
- **5:30–7:45.** Pell offers her promotion and restored subsidy if she certifies that the false messages caused no harm. She refuses the false certification. He warns that staff will be laid off; the budget threat is real.
- **7:45–10:00.** Len produces invoices proving comfort-message licensing and executive retention bonuses can fund the building's transition without cutting clinical shifts. He will testify if residents protect staff wages. **Hook:** tomorrow's public consent hearing is scheduled twelve minutes before the automated annual renewal.

**Character turn:** Mara trades reputation, not just a job opportunity. Isha begins trusting an action rather than an apology.

### Episode 5 — “Have You Tried Being Better?”
**Objective:** get an enforceable interim ruling before the renewal cutoff. **Local outcome:** the renewal is paused; final control depends on Pell authenticating the records.

- **0:00–1:30.** WellSpring's counsel presents a moving testimonial from a patient who needs the Hold. Isha refuses to attack him, damaging the easy “ban the treatment” campaign the audience might expect.
- **1:30–3:30.** Isha publicly discloses her own renewals. “It helped. That is why what you did is worse.” Mara submits her classification work and accepts exposure to professional discipline.
- **3:30–5:30.** Len explains the separate budget lines. Suri presents the original consent clause. The municipal adjudicator can pause the disputed group contract, not dissolve WellSpring; jurisdiction is explicitly limited.
- **5:30–7:45.** Corporate counsel challenges the source of the cancellation instructions. Pell must authenticate them. He asks for a recess, then visits the clinic floor where staff are preparing to absorb unpaid shifts. Len refuses the familiar solution of making carers pay for management's choices.
- **7:45–10:00.** Pell returns and verifies his own signature. The adjudicator pauses automatic renewal and orders the subsidy protected during review. **Hook:** Mara is offered an immediate Hold for the grief she has begun to feel. Now no employer can require it. She must choose.

**Character turn:** the antagonist gives up the right to describe his coercion as kindness. His liability remains unresolved.

### Episode 6 — “Please Stay on the Line”
**Objective:** get through the first unscripted day without replacing one compulsory emotion with another. **Local outcome:** a sustainable local care arrangement begins; the sisters choose presence.

- **0:00–1:30.** Two weeks later, residents choose renew, release, or defer with an independent counselor. Appointments are still inconvenient. An irritated patient wants her old slot, not a liberation speech. The show respects her.
- **1:30–3:30.** The housing supplement has resumed; reserve spending is reconciled separately, escrow remains sealed, and future administrative charges fund paid transition-clinic hours under the interim order. Len keeps working. Pell is suspended, not heroically installed as director of the new service.
- **3:30–5:30.** Mara declines immediate renewal but books support and leaves future treatment open. She begins an ordinary administrative shift at the independent clinic. Forgiveness is uneven; the widower from episode 1 asks her to stop the calls entirely.
- **5:30–7:45.** The sisters play Rena's real final message. It contains no secret plan: a shopping reminder, anger about pain, an apology, and a request to sit together. Isha laughs at the timing. Mara cries; Isha does not. Neither polices the other.
- **7:45–10:00.** Mara puts on the kettle. The building's automated announcement begins, “We hope you are having—” and Suri switches it off for a scheduled quiet hour. Life outside continues noisily. **Final image:** three cups on the table; one remains empty. They drink without clearing it away.

**Closed:** fabricated messages, coerced group renewal, subsidy retaliation, the sisters' avoidance. **Open:** how a society funds care without turning dependency into profit. Their mother stays dead; no recording resolves that loss.

## 7. Continuity and ethical stress test

- **Why not simply stop renewing?** Rebound can be difficult; job and subsidy retaliation make the supposedly free choice coercive. Neither obstacle requires supernatural mind control.
- **Why believe the false recordings?** The service began with real family recordings. Synthetic files inherit that trust and are labeled as archival material. The fraud is about provenance, not citizens failing to spot obvious robots.
- **Why does one hearing matter?** It prevents one pending contract from locking and protects one building. Evidence can support wider action later; the series does not claim instant national reform.
- **Why not abolish the Hold?** It offers useful relief. Abolition would repeat the same removal of consent in a different ideological costume.
- **Mental-health boundary:** the Hold is invented. Do not visually equate real medication or therapy with brainwashing. Obtain informed consultation before scripting clinical procedures.

## 8. Audiovisual and production bible

**Hero prop:** an ugly electric kettle, visible in all six episodes. Not a magical inheritance; an action that requires staying long enough for water to boil.

**Palette:** insured interiors in apricot and mint; canteen in practical steel and warm steam. Do not make sadness literally gray or joy literally fake. Genuine laughter belongs in both environments.

**Sound:** a three-note customer-service chime migrates from punchline to threat. Rena's real audio is textured and unpolished. The finale does not swell into a triumphant cure anthem; leave space around ordinary room sound.

**Camera/animation:** locked, symmetrical service scenes; more shared frames as the sisters reconnect. Large emotional moments play in duration rather than a montage of tears.

**Recurring locations:** claims floor/booth, canteen/kitchen, clinic room, cooperative hall repurposed as hearing room, sisters' apartment. Five principal characters plus the adjudicator and small recurring resident roles. Crowd scale can be conveyed through adjacent booths and off-screen voices.

**Sample voice exchange:**

> MARA: I can get you an extension.  
> ISHA: On Mum being dead?  
> MARA: On the paperwork.  
> ISHA: You always say that like they're different departments.

**What to resist:** a montage in which everyone cries and is therefore fixed. The victory is permission, support, and material protection—not universal catharsis.
