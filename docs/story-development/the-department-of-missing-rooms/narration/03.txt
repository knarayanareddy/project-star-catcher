I began with the dining table. It was secured against theft, although there was nobody allowed to eat at it. Mrs. Rill brought the building's old plans and a socket wrench. She had spent six months asking me to look at those plans. I had advised her to submit a clearer copy.

We removed the family that had never lived there. Their six chairs. Their reassuring lamps. Their pears, which were hollow. The real residents worked without waiting to forgive me. I had confused those two things before: being permitted to help and being declared a good person. Only one was necessary tonight.

Inside the shaft, Lenna had found the release bar. She passed the signal from room to room. Three taps to prepare. A raised hand to wait. The old maintenance diagrams called this internal clearance. My sister had always been good at explaining a difficult thing without making the other person feel small.

We tested the first alignment. The frame moved, caught, and began to strain. I stopped it. It would have been possible to call the noise progress. I know, because I have attended meetings. One partition crossed the receiver line. It had to come down before anything living was asked to pass through it.

Behind the partition was the purge bypass. Stopping a transfer would not stop the morning disposal. That had been designed separately, to prevent delays. The city had anticipated resistance from equipment. It had not, apparently, anticipated anyone wanting to keep the rooms. We had a place for them now. We still had to keep it.
