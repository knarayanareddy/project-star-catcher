At three fifty-five, the feeding valves closed. Then the collector stopped. We did it in that order. I am leaving the order in this account because beautiful stories have a habit of forgetting the instructions that kept everyone alive. The town went dim. The clinic did not. Somewhere a child discovered the window could show more than its own reflection.

I gave the return cue once. With the collector quiet, home was finally somewhere they could go. The filaments loosened. The moths gathered along the guide rail and passed into the nursery. At four we closed the shutters and began the hour. Then I switched off the speaker. I had no fourth word to offer. That was the point of the hour.

Seventeen minutes felt long enough to doubt everything. Teo did not wake. The old reflex offered me a remedy: put the voice back, put the light back, make the room look as though we had done something. We had done something. Now we had to avoid undoing it because waiting looked unimpressive.

A latch slipped. We caught the outer shutter before the inner chamber was exposed. No miracle held it. A person with a tired arm did. We changed places when the arm shook. This is the part of a night people rarely put on monuments: taking over a small necessary task before someone else can no longer manage it.

At five, the hour was complete. We brought one rested bank online and kept the exhausted colony quiet. Teo opened his eyes. I will not improve the moment by saying everyone did. Some needed longer. The first thing he looked for was his headset. I put it out of reach, and then asked myself whether I had learned anything. So I put it on the table and waited.
