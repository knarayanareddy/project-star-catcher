# NOBODY OWNS TUESDAY
## A six-part limited series · 6 × 10 minutes

**Genre:** surreal workplace comedy / intimate institutional heist.  
**Tagline:** Everyone gets a turn. Some people get every night.  
**Logline:** In a city that prevents inherited power by rotating citizens through offices and apartments while suppressing their personal memories, a newly appointed equality inspector discovers that the cleaner she keeps falling in love with belongs to the permanent caste holding the city together—and must stop a scheduled memory consolidation from deleting the evidence of their existence.

**The question:** If everyone gets a turn at power, who does the work that cannot be interrupted?

## 1. The society and its ideological bargain

The **Commonwealth of Turns** emerged from a hereditary corporate city where families owned housing, professions, and public office. Its revolution established universal services, equal pay, and a monthly civic lottery. No citizen could retain a desirable office, prestigious address, or privileged social network indefinitely.

The early reforms worked. Then anti-nepotism became anti-attachment. A fictional memory-interface system, **the Index**, began partitioning autobiographical recall by monthly “turn.” At the changeover, the last month's personal memories are archived and access is blocked. Language, facts, motor skills, and certified professional training remain available. People know the system exists and publicly consent as a condition of equal civic standing; meaningful refusal means permanent assignment to support work outside the lottery.

Citizens rotate **titles and administrative offices**, not untrained surgery or engineering. A rotating hospital chair allocates budgets; permanent licensed clinicians treat patients. This distinction is a central hypocrisy: expertise and continuity have not vanished. They have merely lost public prestige and political voice.

A permanent caste called **the Continuants** maintains utilities, care, archives, and safety. Their nominally temporary exemption became hereditary in practice because children of Continuants are routed into the same training tracks. They receive equal basic wages but cannot enter the office-and-housing lottery, vote in turn-holder assemblies, or accumulate recognized public achievements. The city calls them “transitional support personnel” forty-two years after the transition.

Public slogan: **“No one above. No one left behind.”** Unspoken condition: someone must stay behind to reset the rooms.

### Everyday life

- Moving trucks cross every district on the same morning. A man complains that his third turn as mayor includes the same dead office plant.
- A rotating official solemnly unveils a plaque, then asks which side of the ribbon is public.
- People attend sanctioned “introduction suppers” to establish fresh relationships. A couple finds each other every month without knowing why they order the same meal.
- Rotating officeholders work from permanent technical briefs. Their fresh perspective sometimes catches real abuses; the system is not useless.
- Continuants use nicknames rather than office names. They remember birthdays nobody else knows they have forgotten.

## 2. The speculative mechanism and hard rules

1. **The Index blocks retrieval; it does not initially destroy stored memories.** Past turns remain in an external personal archive until a scheduled consolidation. Physical archives and human testimony therefore matter.
2. **Skills are separate from autobiographical access.** A person can know how to play an instrument without recalling who taught them. Emotional familiarity can persist as an unexplained preference. It cannot spontaneously reveal whole conversations.
3. **A turn lasts twenty-eight days.** This story occurs during the final forty-eight hours of one turn. Ordinary calendar weekdays still exist; “Tuesday” is a recurring meeting name, not a time-loop mechanic.
4. **Citizen IDs persist.** Office badges and public forms foreground turn-names, but the archive can link all turns to one person. The state has not literally lost track of its population.
5. **Only the scheduled civic reset or a clinical retrieval station can change access.** Touching, kissing, music, or extreme stress does not magically restore memory. Those things can motivate investigation, not solve it.
6. **Continuants remember continuously and keep essential qualifications.** They use the same technology for clinical care if desired, but are exempt from compulsory monthly reset because their work depends on continuity.
7. **The upcoming consolidation deletes older personal archives to reduce storage and legal obligations.** It also erases the accumulated evidence that many citizens repeatedly requested to leave the reset regime. Current technical records remain intact; the city's power grid is not stored inside romantic memories.
8. **Reset and consolidation are distinct operations.** A routine reset can be paused without shutting down utilities. The authorities tell citizens they are inseparable because a single Changeover Council authorizes both.
9. **The physical archive has a lawful three-person hold procedure.** An inspector, a records custodian, and a randomly selected citizen witness can preserve disputed data for seventy-two hours. The heist is getting the right people and evidence through the process before the deadline, not inventing an unlimited hack.
10. **Unblocking memory is individual and potentially disorienting.** Any later retrieval must be voluntary, staged, and supported. There will be no citywide blast of memories as a triumphant cure.

## 3. Principal characters

| Character | Contradiction and protective belief | Want / need | Voice and behavior | Final choice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ada, 32**, this turn's equality inspector | Earnest, perceptive, addicted to being exemplary. “If the system gives everyone the same chance, my success cannot hurt anyone.” | Wants a coveted excellence commendation; needs to recognize relationships and labor the metrics exclude. | Reads instructions aloud; makes precise jokes without knowing they are jokes. | **Sol, 35**, Continuant lift technician and night cleaner | Has loved versions of Ada over several turns and stopped asking to be remembered. “A relationship I don't risk cannot be taken away.” | Wants his crew out of invisibility; needs to stop deciding on Ada's behalf that forgetting is kinder. | Understates danger, fixes a hinge while ending a conversation, uses people's persistent names. | Gives Ada access to evidence of her own choices, then asks what she wants now rather than demanding an old promise. |
| **Chair Vey, 57**, Changeover Council supervisor, a permanent Continuant | A former anti-hereditary organizer who made herself indispensable. “Only I remember why equality matters.” | Wants consolidation completed before dissent exposes her caste exemption. | **Bex, 27**, office assistant this turn, bus dispatcher last turn | Loves the lottery because it genuinely widened her world. “Only bitter people want continuity.” | Wants to keep access to interesting work; needs to separate rotation from erasure. | Fast comic improviser, invents job titles when frightened; writes everything on her sleeves. | Becomes the citizen witness selected by a public draw, then advocates voluntary rotation without compulsory forgetting. |

**Naming rule:** citizens keep a persistent personal name. Ada's office badge adds a turn designation; it does not make her a different legal person every month. This avoids a mystery dependent on the government inexplicably failing to recognize her.

### Relationship spine: Ada and Sol

The attraction is present-tense, not proof of destiny. Sol recognizes repeated habits; Ada experiences familiarity she cannot explain. He withholds history to protect himself and avoid pressuring her, but that withholding also limits her agency. In episode 3 she learns she previously chose a lawful reset despite their relationship. In episode 4 she learns that on a later turn she tried to withdraw consent and was administratively blocked. Both facts remain true. In episode 6, with no forced memory restoration, she chooses another Tuesday meeting. Their future is a choice, not a recovered contract.

## 4. Season causality and central reveal

An anonymous fairness complaint sends Ada to a Continuant service lift → she finds repeated inspection signatures under her own ID → Sol directs her to records but withholds their personal history → the archive shows both genuine previous consent and later obstructed refusal → the approaching consolidation threatens the proof → Ada uses the existing hold procedure → Vey tries to disqualify the witness and keep maintenance staff working through the hearing → a planned, safe administrative handover makes the permanent labor visible → a public hold stops deletion and forces separation of voluntary rotation from compulsory erasure.

**The revelation:** the injustice is not that equality itself is a trick. Equal services and the ending of inherited private offices were real achievements. The injustice is that officials declared relationship and continuity dangerous for ordinary people while making both indispensable privileges of the governing caste.

**Clock:** forty-eight hours until consolidation, not an unexplained recurring apocalypse. Ordinary monthly reset has happened many times; this is the first large archive deletion under the new cost-saving directive.

## 5. Six episodes

### Episode 1 — “Congratulations on Your Turn”
**Time remaining:** 48 hours. **Objective:** Ada must close an anonymous fairness complaint before her commendation review. **Local outcome:** she identifies a category of citizens absent from the equality report.

- **0:00–1:30 — Rotating dignity.** An outgoing minister tries to give away an office plant, unaware he inherited it from himself two turns earlier. Ada cites the equal-possession regulation. Bex asks whether the dead leaves count as inherited wealth.
- **1:30–3:30 — The complaint.** “Inspect the lift marked SERVICE.” Ada finds Sol cleaning the same shaft where he maintains its safety systems. He knows her preferred tea. She assumes it is on her hospitality profile; he does not correct her.
- **3:30–5:30 — The absent people.** The official equality dashboard compares lottery-eligible citizens only. Continuants are coded as infrastructure support, outside the measure. Ada first thinks she has found an innocent reporting mistake.
- **5:30–7:45 — A familiar mark.** Inside the lift control cabinet she finds several earlier inspection stickers with her persistent ID. One contains a hand-drawn small blue chair. Sol closes the cabinet before she can inspect the rest.
- **7:45–10:00 — First challenge.** Ada refuses to close the complaint. Vey praises her seriousness and reminds her that monthly attachments can contaminate impartiality. **Hook:** an older sticker says, in Ada's handwriting, “Ask why I withdrew consent.”

**Turn:** she doubts the metric without yet doubting the ideology. **Image:** the inspector's immaculate report excludes the person reflected in its tablet screen.

### Episode 2 — “Transferable Skills”
**Time remaining:** 36 hours. **Objective:** learn why she inspected the same lift in earlier turns. **Local outcome:** she proves personal records exist and gains a retrieval appointment.

- **0:00–1:30.** Ada interviews rotating officeholders. They remember facts about the city but no particular previous inspections. One recites three safety certificates and cannot identify his daughter's favorite food. Treat the absence as unsettling, not a laugh at impaired memory.
- **1:30–3:30.** Sol demonstrates the professional distinction: Ada can read a lift diagram but is not qualified to alter its governor. He has uninterrupted training, not mystical genius. She asks why qualification means exclusion from civic power.
- **3:30–5:30.** Vey explains the revolution's original problem. Families hoarded offices through personal networks. Rotation broke that monopoly; the Index was sold as a way to make each turn genuinely equal. She points to a real former oligarchic residence now shared by citizens.
- **5:30–7:45.** Bex discovers that personal archives require a retrieval appointment, not a password. Ada invokes inspector access to records relevant to the complaint. Eli grants a supervised viewing of one earlier turn. The process has a paper trail Vey can see.
- **7:45–10:00.** The retrieved fragment shows Ada and Sol sitting beside a small blue chair, laughing. It does not restore the full memory. **Hook:** the next record shows Ada signing an apparently valid consent to reset after that relationship began.

**Turn:** Sol's knowledge stops feeling merely tender; Ada recognizes that he has been choosing what she can learn.

### Episode 3 — “Previously, on You”
**Time remaining:** 24 hours. **Objective:** distinguish genuine choice from a story others want Ada to believe about herself. **Local outcome:** she finds the obstructed refusal; Sol admits his withholding.

- **0:00–1:30.** The retrieval scene continues in the same room, with clear record dates. No fake flashback is presented as current reality. Earlier Ada sincerely argued that attachment might bias her next appointment and agreed to reset. The system did not forge that choice.
- **1:30–3:30.** Ada confronts Sol. He admits they met again in later turns and that he stopped volunteering the history because each departure hurt. She tells him, “You don't get to protect me by joining them.” He cannot repair the scene with a kiss.
- **3:30–5:30.** Eli identifies the blue chair as an informal meeting point beside the old Tuesday market. It is a location, not a coded server key. Ada asked Sol to meet there across several turns; some meetings were romantic, one was an argument.
- **5:30–7:45.** A later dated petition shows Ada requesting permanent withdrawal from reset. It was rejected because essential-staff vacancies were said to be unavailable—even though the law allows voluntary continuity. Someone made freedom conditional on a job allocation.
- **7:45–10:00.** Eli reveals the consolidation order: older petitions and personal archives will be deleted tomorrow, while technical logs remain. **Hook:** his own signature approves the deletion schedule.

**Turn:** the mystery becomes collective and evidentiary, not just a romance about a stolen past.

### Episode 4 — “Essential, Temporarily”
**Time remaining:** 12 hours. **Objective:** preserve admissible evidence under the three-person hold procedure. **Local outcome:** Ada and Eli agree to act, but they still need an independent citizen witness.

- **0:00–1:30.** Eli opens his unofficial fragments collection: petition numbers, hearing dates, partial recordings. Ada refuses to rely on a secret collection controlled by one sympathetic man. It must be authenticated against official originals.
- **1:30–3:30.** She reads the hold procedure aloud. An inspector initiates, a custodian verifies, and a citizen drawn from the active registry witnesses. The witness may refuse; selection alone cannot compel consent. This rule prevents Ada from simply appointing Sol.
- **3:30–5:30.** Sol and the Continuant crews arrange a **safe handover demonstration**: noncritical office resets pause, while power, lifts, clinical care, and water remain staffed. They will not endanger residents to prove they matter. The city begins seeing who actually resets its prestigious rooms.
- **5:30–7:45.** Vey offers Ada a permanent Continuant supervisor post with full memory access if she closes the complaint. It would let her keep Sol and her professional standing. She refuses to make their relationship a private exemption purchased with everyone else's forgetting.
- **7:45–10:00.** A public mechanical draw selects Bex as witness. She is thrilled until she learns participating may delay her next turn as a cultural commissioner. **Hook:** Vey challenges her impartiality because she currently works for Ada.

**Turn:** Ada rejects becoming the exceptional person the system permits to remember. Bex must decide whether equality means only her next opportunity.

### Episode 5 — “Everybody Gets a Turn”
**Time remaining:** 90 minutes. **Objective:** validate the witness and reach the physical archive terminal before consolidation. **Local outcome:** the hold is initiated but its authentication must be made public to survive administrative cancellation.

- **0:00–1:30.** The challenge hearing starts in an office with no chairs because its Continuant reset team is demonstrating next door. The comedy reveals labor, not the supposed stupidity of rotating citizens.
- **1:30–3:30.** Bex proves selection followed the published process; the regulation excludes family and financial beneficiaries, not temporary colleagues. She declares her interest anyway and consents to public scrutiny. Vey cannot change the rule retrospectively without making the abuse explicit.
- **3:30–5:30.** Ada, Bex, and Eli travel through the service route, with Sol maintaining access as part of a logged escort. There is no security chase requiring implausible physical prowess. The obstacles are closed departments, missing authorizations, and a locked public lift whose actual technician is standing beside them.
- **5:30–7:45.** Vey issues an order recalling Eli to ordinary deletion duty. He refuses and enters the archive authentication. Ada initiates the seventy-two-hour hold. The terminal requires witness confirmation and a public notification to the Changeover assembly.
- **7:45–10:00.** The assembly feed is still advertising “a fresh start.” Nima-like hacking is not imported: Bex invokes the witness's reserved notification slot under the same procedure. **Hook:** she has sixty seconds to explain why citizens should pause the reset they believe keeps them equal.

**Turn:** Eli gives up safe private preservation; Bex becomes more than Ada's assistant. Their legitimacy comes from exposing themselves to scrutiny.

### Episode 6 — “Tuesday, If You Want”
**Time remaining:** minutes until the consolidation authorization. **Objective:** make the hold enforceable and separate memory choice from civic rank. **Local outcome:** deletion stops, public records survive, and an interim citizen process replaces unilateral supervision.

- **0:00–1:30.** Bex uses the minute to show two records: Ada's genuine earlier consent and her later rejected refusal. “A choice you can make only once isn't a turn.” She does not tell citizens that their current lives or relationships are unreal.
- **1:30–3:30.** The feed displays the completed three-party hold. Witnessed notification makes unilateral cancellation unlawful. The assembly declines the combined authorization and demands separate votes on routine reset and consolidation. Essential systems continue operating, visibly disproving Vey's claim that both must happen together.
- **3:30–5:30.** The seventy-two-hour preservation triggers an emergency public review already provided for in the rules. Ada presents the denominator trick excluding Continuants. Sol speaks for his crew's demands: civic standing, training access, relief staffing, and voluntary memory policy—not a demand that everybody become a technician overnight.
- **5:30–7:45.** Three months later, an interim charter ends compulsory erasure as a condition of office. Voluntary retrieval clinics open slowly; a minority still chooses reset, and Bex still chooses rotating work. Continuants enter the civic draw; safety posts remain qualified and staffed. Vey faces review for blocked petitions. Eli's records enter a publicly accountable archive.
- **7:45–10:00.** Ada has chosen not to restore all her old turns yet. She waits at the blue chair on Tuesday. Sol arrives and starts to say what they used to order, then stops and asks what she would like. She chooses something different. **Final image:** the camera pulls back to show the chair bolted to a maintenance hatch. Sol laughs: “We really should move it.” Together, they do.

**Closed:** consolidation, concealment of refusals, Continuants' legal invisibility, the private-exemption temptation. **Open:** the best balance between rotating opportunity and accumulated responsibility. The relationship begins again without asserting that a previous self owns the present one.

## 7. Logic and emotional stress test

- **Why hasn't nobody noticed they lose memories?** They know and are taught to value it. The hidden facts are the obstructed refusals, permanent hierarchy, and excluded denominator—not the existence of reset.
- **Why do permanent staff tolerate the arrangement?** They retain continuity, family ties, qualifications, and stable housing, but face civic exclusion and retaliation. Some prefer their position to the lottery; they are not a unanimous revolutionary bloc.
- **Why haven't former inspectors stopped this before?** Complaints were delayed beyond turn-end and petitioners were reset. The approaching consolidation creates urgency, while Eli's accumulating indexed evidence and a correctly witnessed hold supply a stronger route this time. Ada is not the first person to care or the sole source of resistance.
- **Why can Ada meet Sol repeatedly?** Civic rotation changes office assignments and apartments, not the whole city's geography. Sol services the same district; the chair sits at a public weekly market. Recurrence is likely, not cosmically guaranteed.
- **Why not retrieve all memories immediately?** Consent, clinical capacity, and identity conflict matter. An archive is evidence of a person's history, not a command they must obey.
- **Why not keep Ada permanently in charge?** That would reproduce Vey's exceptional guardianship. Ada's authority expires; a fixed-term, mixed review body replaces unilateral supervision. Expertise advises under accountable mandates.
- **Why is reform possible within three months?** The immediate victory is preservation and suspension of compulsory reset. Structural labor relief and training remain ongoing commitments, not magically completed promises.

## 8. Audiovisual and production bible

**Hero prop:** the small blue chair. It is furniture before it is a symbol. Its repeated location makes disjointed time legible; moving it together is a modest rejection of predetermined repetition.

**Palette and transitions:** identical sets change through color-coded turn banners, badge overlays, plant condition, and rearranged furniture. Service infrastructure keeps a stable ochre/blue palette. The unsettling effect comes from people calling the rearranged room a fresh start while the same worn floor marks remain.

**Sound:** cheerful handover fanfare; lift relays; a half-hummed melody Ada can play procedurally but cannot place autobiographically. The melody does not unlock anything. In the finale, Sol and Ada fail to agree on its ending and laugh.

**Animation grammar:** match cuts across turns, repeated blocking with altered interpersonal distance, visible dates on retrieved footage. No kaleidoscopic effects that obscure whether a record is current, simulated, or retrieved.

**Locations:** one civic office redressed, service lift/corridor, archive room, public assembly/feed stage, Tuesday market/chair corner. A single street can imply a whole city through simultaneous moving carts and uniform changeover sounds. Memory retrieval uses replayed scenes with contextual changes, not expensive alternate-world environments.

**Sample voice exchange:**

> ADA: Why aren't you eligible for a turn?  
> SOL: I'm essential.  
> ADA: That sounds like a compliment.  
> SOL: It was, forty-two years ago.

**What to resist:** the romance must not become proof that love overrides consent. Sol's best act is giving Ada the information and room to make a decision he cannot guarantee.
