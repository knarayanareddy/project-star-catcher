I stopped the loop. Harl showed me the spare. Of course there was a spare. We had planned carefully for the possibility that comfort might fail to arrive on time. We had not planned for the possibility that it might keep arriving after someone no longer wanted it. I put both recordings where we could see them.

Exhausted scales came away from the wings. For a moment the corridor filled with things people had dreamed: a dog, an open door, someone standing at the wrong end of a childhood table. They were not visitors. They were impressions. I say this because grief is very willing to mistake resemblance for permission to believe.

I told the town there were thirty-one people in extended feeding. I did not say they were peacefully resting. I did not promise they would all wake the moment we did the right thing. I said the light had a cost we had stopped reporting. It was the least soothing announcement of my career.

People were frightened. Some were angry. They brought lists. Machines that could not simply stop, journeys that still had to happen, a relative whose breathing depended on a socket. Their questions were not proof that they loved the system. They were the work required if I meant what I had just asked them to consider.

The window-makers had copies of Teo's notes. He had not been waiting for one important person to understand him. He had been doing the work with other people. They started checking the shutters. I found myself standing in the way, holding a microphone. For once I could improve the situation by moving aside.
